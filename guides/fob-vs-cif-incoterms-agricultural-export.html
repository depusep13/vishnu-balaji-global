FOB vs CIF Incoterms for Indian Agricultural Exports

Deciding between Free on Board (FOB) and Cost, Insurance & Freight (CIF). How international buyers can optimize ocean freight rates, marine cargo insurance, and customs risk.

Authored by Sandeep Solanke
Scope: ICC Incoterms® 2020 Rules

1. The Core Differences at a Glance

When contracting agricultural shipments from India (such as 1121 Basmati Rice, Wheat, Pulses, or Fresh Produce), selecting the right Incoterm dictates who negotiates ocean freight, who purchases marine cargo insurance, and exactly when risk transfers from seller to buyer:

Term Seller Responsibility Buyer Responsibility Risk Transfer Point
FOB (Free on Board) Origin transport, packing, customs clearance, loading on ship Ocean freight, marine insurance, destination port clearance Once loaded on board the vessel at Indian port
CFR (Cost & Freight) Origin logistics, Indian customs, and ocean freight to destination Marine cargo insurance, destination customs, inland carriage Once loaded on board vessel in India (despite seller paying freight)
CIF (Cost, Insurance & Freight) Origin logistics, customs, ocean freight, and marine cargo insurance Destination port clearance, import duty, final inland transport Once loaded on board vessel at Indian origin port

2. When Should an Importer Choose FOB?

Purchasing under FOB Indian Port (e.g., FOB Mundra or FOB Nhava Sheva) is typically advantageous when:

  • The buyer maintains established global volume service contracts with major container carriers (such as Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, or Hapag-Lloyd) and receives preferential ocean freight rates.
  • The buyer prefers using their nominated freight forwarder to control transit scheduling, free demurrage days at the discharge port, and shipping line container tracking.

3. When Should an Importer Choose CIF or CFR?

Purchasing under CIF or CFR Destination Port is often the preferred strategy when:

  • The buyer wants an all-inclusive, fixed landed cost per Metric Ton without exposure to sudden ocean freight surcharges, bunker adjustments (BAF), or peak season surcharges (PSS).
  • The exporter leverages high-volume container freight contracts from Indian ports to negotiate lower sea freight rates than the overseas importer can obtain individually.
  • The buyer prefers a single invoice covering commodity sourcing, fumigation, phytosanitary inspection, and maritime transit up to destination port gates.
